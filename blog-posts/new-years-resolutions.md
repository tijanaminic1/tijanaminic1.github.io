# New Year's Resolutions And Why I Think They Matter

## Magical whimsical feel of childhood 

There are a few things left in our adult life that truly transport us into the state of mind we used to have as children. That eagerness, curiosity, genuine excitement about mundane things in life. The internet overused the phrase "childlike sense of wonder", but it really is a beautiful way to describe that fleeting, hard-to-name feeling we sometimes catch in the most unexpected moments.

I’ve found that writing New Year’s resolutions captures that feeling surprisingly well. They’re a quiet promise to yourself that you’re still trying. You look forward to a new beginning, get excited at the prospect of living a more fulfilled life, and allow yourself to fantasize and use your imagination, much like a child would. People often scoff at the mention of New Year's resolutions, usually pointing out that "most people don't even stick to them". That's true. But even if no concrete change happens *this* year, it is important to take the time to reflect and deeply think about the things in our lives that need improvement. Or simply, areas in their life that require more nurture and care.

And honestly — do you realize how many people *never* stop to question themselves at all? Who never pause to reflect on the direction of their life, or whether it aligns with what they actually need? In that light, the resolution isn’t naïve, but tremendously brave and admirable.

## Why write about it?

In this post, I’ll share some of my New Year’s resolutions, in the hope that they might inspire someone or give them ideas for their own list. Another reason I wanted to write this is that I’ve been noticing how easy it has become for many of us to lose a sense of discipline, often because our attention is constantly pulled in different directions, or because self-doubt makes improvement feel intimidating. Sometimes it’s easier to assume we won’t succeed than to risk trying at all. I’ve also noticed a rise in activities labeled as self-care that don’t always serve us in meaningful ways. Through sharing my own list, I want to show that many of my resolutions are deeply rooted in genuine self-care — the kind that supports long-term well-being, rather than just short-term comfort.


## 2025 Resolutions — How did I do?

The first time I actually wrote down my New Year’s resolutions was at the end of 2024. Every other time, I would make them, but then forget what they were, which usually resulted in not completing them. This time, I was a bit smarter about it — ten resolutions, a round, doable number, with the goal of sticking to at least half. I tried to make most of them quantifiable, so I could clearly decide whether they were a success or a failure. I managed to succeed in six of them (seven, if you decide to count one unquantifiable one).

Then I thought about how my year went. I think 2025 was arguably one of the hardest years of my life. I experienced things I never thought I would, and I learned immensely from them, but they were not easy to navigate — in the moment, or after the fact. Still, sticking to those specific resolutions improved my quality of life by a margin.

One of them, for example, was to read at least ten books purely for enjoyment. I ended up coming back to reading after years of not doing it consistently. By the end of the year, I had read more than ten books. My attention span improved, and I found something to fill my time while battling doomscrolling withdrawal. I also made it a goal to work out every week (not including periods of travel, when I didn’t have access to a gym) and to eat only home-cooked meals — meaning no DoorDash. I don’t even need to explain how much better that has made me feel.

Another resolution was to spend as much time as possible with my boyfriend, whom I’ve been long-distance with since the end of 2024. Safe to say, out of the 93 thousand miles I flew this year, probably a third of them were just for the purpose of seeing him. And while that resolution was costly — both in time and money — it helped me get through the start of my PhD by allowing me to see one of my emotional support people every three-ish weeks. And, of course, knowing that we're able to navigate the extended period of long distance and show up for each other in this way helped elevate my relationship to the next level.

In summary, I think I did pretty well. This inspired me to make a list this year.


## Some of the items on my 2026 list

### Try to state a true intention behind everything you do

Like a true Virgo, I hate wasting time (and if you make fun of me for liking astrology, you lack the ability to be whimsical and silly). A lot of my day is spent battling the urge to do meaningless things that hand me a dopamine boost on a silver platter.

What’s interesting is how many of those things we don’t even register as distractions. We justify them in vague, comforting ways, but most of the time we’re just lying to ourselves. My least favorite justification is when people defend their doomscrolling habits by saying, “I like to stay informed,” or “I learn so much useful stuff there.” Sure — that might be true for maybe ten percent of the time. But we both know that most of it is just being force-fed outrage, frustration, and noise until you’re emotionally exhausted.

I’ve been thinking a lot about how to filter out all the noise that prevents me from going to bed feeling like I actually accomplished something that day — whether that’s completing a challenging task, spending quality time with friends or family, or connecting with myself. I decided to try writing down the intention behind every action I plan to take that day, in as much detail as possible. By intention, I mean what I want to take away from the task — something *concrete*. This way of navigating the day doesn't necessarily take away spontaneity. Sometimes, spontaneity is the intention itself. But I think it's important to be able to identify it.

For example, if I’m working on a programming task, I can’t always anticipate exactly what I’ll learn. But I *can* decide what I *want* to learn that day. It doesn’t matter if it’s something small. As long as the intention is there and I manage to fulfill it, I’ll feel accomplished — and then I can set a new intention. The task itself becomes meaningful, and that makes it deserving of my time.

The same applies to social plans. If I go out with people and set an intention that the purpose of the hangout is to connect with them, one of two things happens. Either I actively work to make that time valuable — I listen more closely, engage more intentionally, and try to make them feel comfortable. Success. Or I realize that I don’t actually *want* to connect with these particular people (for whatever reason), which saves me the time and energy of living out a filler episode.

And tell me — how would you even begin to justify this intention: *“For the next three hours, I’m going to lie in bed and scroll through apps, searching for nothing in particular, taking in whatever they decide to show me today.”* Exactly. You can't really. So you realize that it takes away much more than it gives, and you can drop it and go off to do something of value.

> This resolution will probably be the hardest one to stick to. It’s an incredibly challenging habit to build — I catch myself multiple times a day automatically reaching for my phone and opening YouTube Shorts (this is what happens when you delete TikTok *and* Instagram). Rewiring my brain won’t be easy, but I’m genuinely excited about the possibility of succeeding.

### Discover at least 10 cool places in Seattle

I’m sure there are more than ten, but the point of this resolution was to force myself to leave the house more often. I’ve been a social butterfly my whole life, but over the past couple of years I’ve withdrawn and become more of a homebody. I suspect that has a lot to do with constantly being on the move and craving some sense of stability.

Since moving to Seattle a few months ago, I’ve barely explored any of the city’s wonders, which I find pretty tragic. It’s the first time in my life that I’m living in an actual urban city — not just on the periphery — and I’m not really making an effort to get to know it.

And beyond just *seeing* places, *experiencing* different cultures has been invaluable to me throughout my life. So why am I not actively working toward that now? This resolution is about stopping myself from using work as an excuse to stay inside, and instead integrating into whatever the city has to offer.

### Relearn German

Full transparency: I’m not a fan of the German language. I had to learn it in school for ten years and never really grew to like it. But with recent developments in my educational journey, I’ve decided to come back to it. The reason is simple — there’s *too much* of it still stuck in my brain, but not enough for me to confidently say that I *know* it. And that frustrates me deeply.

While dealing with the tremendous amount of catching up I have to do in the discipline I’m currently pursuing a degree in, I’ve started to realize that repetition is unavoidable when it comes to learning. No matter how well you think you understand something when it’s explained to you, you forget, and you *have* to go back and review. Constantly. The number of times I’ve had to Google the actual definition of an API at this point is honestly embarrassing.

That realization made me appreciate the knowledge that did stick. And a surprising amount of German has, even though I haven’t actively used it since graduating from high school. So I’ve decided to rebuild on top of it. Since I can’t get rid of it, I might as well make the space it occupies in my brain useful.


### Stop using the word hate

Another thing I realized this year is that I don’t actually hate anything — or anyone. And yet, interestingly, I’ve been using the word “hate” constantly to describe things that are, more often than not, simply *frustrating*.

Given the state of the world right now, I’ve started thinking more seriously about what the word hate really means. To me, it implies destruction. And for that reason alone, I think it should be used very carefully.

I can say that I’m extremely frustrated with slow walkers, because I’m naturally a fast walker and being forced to slow down makes me feel uncomfortable in my own shoes (pun absolutely intended). I can say that certain aspects of the current social climate frustrate me, because they clash with my own vision of a world where people are happier and things are done differently. And I can say that an ex-friend frustrates me, because they bring up conflicting emotions — disappointment over how things ended, mixed with genuine yearning for all the good memories we had.

What I’m trying to say is that *real* hate comes from within — from an inner desire for destruction that can project itself onto anything and anyone. It latches like an annoying bug to whatever it encounters first, because it doesn’t care what it’s targeting, as long as it’s being fueled. I don’t think most of us actually nurture this kind of emotion, at least not at first.

What I do think is that most of us feel deeply frustrated by a myriad of different things. Our nervous systems get triggered, our core values shaken, our perspectives abruptly disrupted, and that’s not a pleasant experience. But we don’t want to destroy everything that causes that discomfort. That would be like throwing a grenade at the sun because it’s too hot outside — pointless, exhausting, and likely to create a much bigger problem down the line (assuming the grenade somehow succeeds in destroying the sun).

Instead, what we usually do in that situation is react to the discomfort. We turn inward, figure out what we need to function or even thrive in that environment, and then do it — go to the beach, sit under the AC, carry an umbrella. We are forced to coexist with so many things that it's hard to tell at this point which of those things benefit us short- and long-term. Sometimes we can find ways to mitigate the effect of whatever's bugging us, and sometimes we are forced to build up tolerance. But hey, at least we become more resilient and enduring. That's a step-up in our evolution journey.

I believe that eliminating the word hate from my vocabulary will help remind me of all of this. By forcing myself to replace it with something more precise, I can learn to better identify what exactly bothers me about a specific person, situation, or circumstance. And once I can name it more accurately, I can find more effective ways to deal with it. Lumping everything under the umbrella of hate does my problem-solving abilities a disservice.

### Get a drivers licence

I have no desire to. I love being driven by others, and I love well-developed public transportation. But I promised my boyfriend — my biggest victim when it comes to chauffeur duties for the past few years — that I’d get my license. And if the sacrifices we make for the people we love include learning a new skill, one that will serve me for the rest of my life and is now about as essential as knowing how to do laundry, then I’ll happily do it.

The point of including this resolution is to address the growing resistance toward doing things for others that might feel mildly uncomfortable for us. There’s nothing wrong with being a little selfless. I understand that dynamics are shifting and that many of us are trying to protect ourselves from being unknowingly exploited or taken advantage of. What I’m trying to say with this resolution is that, when framed differently, it becomes much easier to see how the things we do for others can also benefit us.

I often fear that, as a woman, I constantly have to be wary of making the wrong decisions that lead to majorly reducing my independence. Some of these fears are justified — others are just projections that come out of the struggle to trust myself to do right by me. And that's okay.

This was, by the way, one of the resolutions I failed to accomplish last year. I’m not making any promises this time — but, as I mentioned in the very first paragraph, I do at least promise myself to try. :-)
