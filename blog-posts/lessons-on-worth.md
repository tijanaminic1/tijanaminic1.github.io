# A deeply uncomfortable conversation about self-worth



## When the outside world goes quiet and you cannot avoid yourself anymore

It has been incredibly hard for me to write this year. I've been writing more than ever, but I've had an enormous influx of thoughts and lessons that I had to digest, and maneuvering with them in a coherent way has been one of the most challenging parts of my adulthood thus far. I turn 25 in less than a month, and they say it's the age your prefrontal cortex is finally done baking, so maybe that's why my brain's been working extra hard these past couple of months.

This year was the first ever time I openly confronted the concept of self-worth. It's been present as a concept I'd hear others talk about, but I always just assumed I already had it — I considered myself a confident person, at least compared to most people around me. While I self-analyzed in ways that were only motivated by wanting to reduce different feelings of discomfort that I would encounter growing up, I never got around to doing those long looks in the mirror and contemplating whether I *truly* love the person I see. 

A few things happened that made me finally stop and evaluate the essence I've been carrying with me my whole life. The first thing creeped in and brought with it a deep existential crisis. There was a moment this year that I woke up and realized I have accomplished every wild ambition I had in all the domains of life I could think of (at least the ones I've set thus far) — I had a wonderful group of friends (something I've struggled with growing up), a wonderful boyfriend who loved me, happy and healthy family, and a career that I advanced in ways that I never thought would be possible, given where I've started. Many more things, of course. It seemed to me in that moment that I could just continue doing what I've been doing, and trying to "beat humanity" and constantly advance myself, but the movement I was detecting didn't seem to lead anywhere — if anything, it was just leading to more ambitions that weren't driven by what I felt in my heart I wanted, but rather the natural order of things, done out of respect for what I'd already accomplished. I felt like nothing was really worth doing anymore.

The second thing that happened in a way was a product of the first, but it advanced it even further. I turned inwards and shut down all external stimuli that would keep me distracted and away from myself. I moved in alone, to a new city where I knew no one, and shut down most of my social media. I limited short-form content and, suddenly, there was much more silence in my everyday life. The only voices I could hear constantly were the ones in my head, and, having been silenced for a very long time, they went rampant on me. With no distractions, lots of pressure from the outside world, and an already present existential crisis, I started to understand the gravity and depth of how I deal with problems, how my brain operates, and my *humanity*.

The third thing, the realest and the easiest one to notice, has been external events that in some way changed the idyllic structure of my life — struggles of doing a PhD, crippling self-doubt, feeling lonely even though I had friends and family, the end of my 4 year relationship, and the fear of the unknown future going forward. These events and feelings extended way beyond my emotional regulation abilities, and all I knew at the time is that I was sad and paralyzed, and that there has to be a way to get unstuck and learn how to accept whatever this vague thing was that was troubling me, keeping me from living the fulfilling life that I was hoping to find at the end of the tunnel.

## Language matters

It took me a while to discover what the problem was, and doing a PhD has taught me that the hardest part of solving a problem is being able to state the problem itself. Trying to recall the exact discovery process seems impossible and unrealistic, as I would assume different people land differently at the same spot. But, the important thing is that, at some point, I realized that I cannot describe a very complex, abstract problem, that has a lot to do with naming your feelings, describing your behaviors, and delving into your personal philosophy, without being extremely precise with the choice of language (something, again, that I am learning during the PhD). 

With a lot of trial and error, I identified four main things that are keeping me stuck.

### Guilt and shame

I spent a lot of this year dissecting the difference between guilt and shame. I thought I spent most of my life constantly feeling guilty about something. We're told that guilt is not good, that if you feel guilty about something it is probably because you have something to apologize for, or that if you haven't done something wrong, you wouldn't have felt guilt. For me, the problem came when I could not possibly identify what I feel guilty about. I stopped drinking alcohol for a good few years because my hangovers would consist of me thinking that I am a terrible person, no matter how peaceful and/or fun the night before was. I would feel guilty for doing human things, making honest mistakes, or simply for being myself in moments where I don't have it 100% confirmed externally that it was safe and "okay" for me to be me.

Once I started thinking about the language I consistently use and its effect, I learned that what I was feeling was not guilt at all, but shame that came from completely unrelated feelings of self-inadequacy. Separating the two helped me move through life with much more ease, and approach people with an open heart and recognizing when someone truly needs forgiveness, and when they need something else entirely. I also learned to extend the same grace to myself when I can.

The way I look at it is this: guilt makes you act, shame keeps you paralyzed. Guilt comes from a deed that does not align with our engraved moral compass, and it leads to a counteraction — an apology, attempt of repair, or moving on and knowing the guilt-inducing action should not be repeated. Guilt is a one-and-done. Shame, on the other hand, is independent of actions; it is a projection of self-worth onto anything it reaches, which sometimes includes actions. When I would feel this "unexplainable guilt", I would try to backtrack and reason through anything that I have done and latch that shame onto the action to justify feeling inadequate and small. But shame, unlike guilt, is stagnant. Nothing comes out of shame. For me, it would make me punish myself and torture myself emotionally so I "repay" my debt to the universe for just being human and imperfect, and sometimes for not even having done anything, but deeming myself unworthy of being happy in that moment. Learning this liberated me in ways that I cannot begin to explain, and helped me be immensely more understanding and less resentful to other people.

### Having to earn it

The talk of unworthiness to be happy brings me to the second thing that I learned after years of internal struggle. I was very fearful of success in any domain of life, as I was entirely convinced that everything comes with a price that I have yet to pay. Basically, the way I saw it was: an opportunity of any kind comes along, I have to grab it, and once I do I discover how much it costs and I have to pay to have it. Because I am not stupid enough to waste potential and opportunity, this led me to feel undeserving of most things that came my way, and to overwork myself mentally and emotionally trying to earn them. This was the root cause of my anxieties about friendships, relationships, my insane imposter syndrome. I lived in constant fear that if I didn't overcompensate hard enough, everything I had would crumble and disappear, and the only worse feeling than not having something is having it and then losing it.

It took a lot of suffering, and conscious language modifications, to reframe how I understood deserving and worthiness. This is my current understanding of it: I don't need to earn anything, I already prove myself worthy of it by even seeing the opportunity and taking it. I see it now as all of us being in a big room full of doors. We are all free to open them and walk through, but not all of us see all doors. People who weren't deserving of a given door didn't even see it in the first place. Deserving, in that sense, is entirely human-controlled: I decide what I deserve, and no one else can decide it for me, because everyone's standards are driven by their own moral compass. Someone might look at a rich person and say they deserve their money simply because they did what it took to get it; someone else, working from a different moral compass, would say the same person is undeserving because of how they got it. Neither is wrong, they're just applying different standards, which is exactly why the judgment has to be mine to make, not anyone else's. 

And it works both ways, since opportunities are neither good or bad, but always neutral. That's why you can't apply any standard for *deserving* it, in a traditional sense of doing anything beyond taking the opportunity. What *appears* bright and shiny might actually end up being a painful lesson. Does that make you any less deserving of it? Regardless, that lesson is also an opportunity *for growth*. And as we grow, the doors we're able to see change too. Regardless of what the room we walk into next looks like, it's the exact room we need to be in that moment.

Accepting this made me able to embrace the good things already in front of me. Everything I can see is there for me, otherwise I wouldn't be able to see it. Many people pass those same doors with their eyes closed. Might as well embrace the beauty and not be afraid of reaching for it.

### Signals and values

This one actually turned me into a cynic for a brief period of time, just because I was so frustrated that I could see it clearly and many around me couldn't. I grew up in a very small town on the outskirts of Belgrade, Serbia. I moved through vastly different standards of living, education, cultures, and overall perspectives on life. I lived in a first-world western country, war-torn Balkan country, and a middle-eastern country built on a mix of tradition and modernity. I had the pleasure of knowing people from every possible side of every spectrum, and I was even luckier to be able to observe their reactions to each other.

What someone signals versus the value they actually carry are extremely different, and so many of us fall prey to that trap. I needed another language lesson to learn the difference between the two. My current circle is mostly people in tech, who many outsiders consider to be an enigma, these smart folks who solve hard problems and currently control the world in a sense. That used to intimidate the shit out of me, especially since I didn't grow up knowing many people like this. This sounds very intuitive, but it's extremely hard to internalize — just because someone is smart for one thing doesn't mean that transcends anything beyond that one thing. They might not be smart in other ways, or a more valuable person by any other measure, at least according to your own compass. Just because someone has money doesn't mean they're hardworking. It's a very simple concept, yet an incredible amount of us fall for it, wrapped in different shiny paper each time. Many intellectuals I know fall for these signals too. A classic one: just because someone is nice, or verbally agrees with some "correct" views, doesn't mean their everyday deeds follow the same underlying values. If anything, I've witnessed the opposite much more often.

Once I realized this, rarely anyone intimidates me anymore. I don't care about what they signal, and most of the time I don't care about their values beyond the extent of the kind of relationship I want to have with them. And it helps me separate my own value from their actions towards me. This helped me a lot in grad school. I can get a lot out of my relationship with my advisors because I am not in the slightest intimidated by them. I don't crumble under their criticism or feedback. I can raise my hand in any class, talk to any person, and seek out opportunities that might seem scary because of the people that guard them. The people that still do intimidate me do so because they possess values that I have yet to build — they seem larger than what I know, but cheap billboards that are there to distract from the real thing do not interest me in the slightest.

### Detach from the outcome, focus on the process

I thank my dad for this one, and now I preach it to every PhD friend that ever complains to me about the hardships of grad school, or anyone really who complains about anything. Good and bad things happen to all of us, all the time, as we live and grow. When I first started school, my dad would always parrot it in my head to not care about grades and only focus on doing my best. *The grades would come*. Don't determine your career by how much you think you'll make, focus on being the best at it. *The money would come*. That's the underlying message: outcome versus process. I struggle with control, so this was a hard one for me to learn. Things can go a million different ways, and it's hard to live life focused only on the outcome: on getting there, on the milestone, on the specific thing. The outcome of the ideal situation is just our projection of how *we think we would feel* if it came true, but we don't actually know how it would feel, because every outcome comes at a different cost, and if we overlook what we truly want, that cost sometimes exceeds the true value of the outcome.

Take dating, for example. I could theoretically end up with a guy I was determined to date, but at the cost of giving him way too much of my time and changing myself in ways that misalign with my values just so I could call him mine. We become blind to what we sacrifice because our sacrifices aren't based on the underlying need, but on the projection of that onto one of the million possible outcomes. I want love, commitment, and stability. I attached those feelings onto this random dude, and my happiness, the aforementioned needs, don't only lie in that single outcome, and with that cost, probably don't lie in that outcome *at all*.

The same logic applies to my PhD. I'm in the program because I want to be able to think about something very deeply and for a very long time, not because I need a degree or a qualification for a job. Once I lose sight of that, I'm screwed — I panic when I get a bunch of criticism from my advisors because it seems like it's pulling me away from my "goal," when in reality it *is* making me a better researcher in many regards, which is the actual goal, not submitting a single paper or passing the quals. Sometimes, though, your advisor is just an asshole, and that's a perfect opportunity to learn how to push back, to stand up for yourself. An admirable quality to learn in any environment, and a great one to add to that *unwritten* resume, in my opinion much more important than the resume you actually send for job applications. I'd rather stick around for as long as I can and acquire as much wisdom and then NOT finish the degree, than get the degree but not retain the skills I came for. The milestone itself is just a poor way to quantify an underlying value, something that's acquired differently for everyone, and progress is much harder to see when the milestones are external rather than internal.

Learning the difference between outcome and process helped me finally master the art of detachment. I am going to be okay no matter where I end up. As long as I act in the interest of my core needs and aspirations, without letting them be defined by external validation and outcomes, I can make the best decisions and trust they'll lead me where I want to be, without my happiness depending on *what happens*.

Timothee Chalamet put it well during the *Dune* press tour:

> You could be the master of your fate, you could be the captain of your soul, but you have to realize that life is coming from you and not at you, and that takes time.

Another line that says something similar, more concisely and poetically, is:

> Let love find you in your living, not your longing.

I think that second one transcends much more than just love. Longing is okay, but understanding that sometimes the things we long for are already within us in some way can set us free. Relationships ending can depict that beautifully. Once someone walks away, you get a very clear picture of how the soul exchange works — a lot of what you think you lost because another person left is actually still within you, and some of it was always there. If the light's within you, it won't leave just because someone else did.

That's what detachment actually means, and I used to think life-coaches talking about it were full of shit. Who could possibly be happy if something bad happens to them? I was trivializing the concept. The power lies in separating what we need from the outcome we think fulfills that need. We act like architects of our own destiny, when in reality we are only the architects of our mind, and destiny just follows.

## What does all of this have to do with self-worth?

A lot, actually. Acting within any of these frameworks is an act of self-love. Recognizing that our happiness and fulfillment transcend circumstances and the traps our mind set for us is within itself recognizing the inherent worth we carry and should allow to freely exist. 

I spent today alone, mostly just meditating and thinking about the things I struggle with at the moment. I am going through a somewhat hard period where it is not yet clear to me what the lesson is. I am beyond the point where I run away from bad periods; I try to embrace them while they're there and find the beauty in pain too. Though, as I go through more stuff, I am realizing that it's just leveling up and the problems to tackle become increasingly abstract and internal, and much less to do with anything bad happening on the outside. And they take much longer to solve.

After meditating, I took a shower and started washing my hair. In that moment, it was the first time in my life that I felt an ever-present feeling of self-love. The best way I could describe it was that it felt like I had a lover beside me, washing my hair instead of me in that moment — except it was just me. It felt that way because the love seemed to be flowing outward, toward someone else, but it was actually returning to me. I felt as if someone was there for me. But the person that *was* there and that felt the love in the moment was me! I managed to connect with myself so deeply, to truly soothe myself and not just not be bored, but genuinely enjoy my own company, in such a mundane thing that is taking a shower. I did not feel alone in that moment, even if there was no one else in the world with me.

That moment alone motivated me to actually write about this process. I have to be there for a lot of people right now, and sometimes it is overwhelming to just talk about problems in the external sense and not dig a little deeper and try to solve the underlying fears and paralyses. 

Knowing that you have yourself and that you truly care about yourself and love yourself as you would a friend or a family member is the most liberating feeling that goes beyond anything bad that could happen, and even if something does, it feels indescribably less scary. It also helps find those better doors that we don't see because we immediately assume we don't deserve them. Most importantly for me, it helps me feel a better sense of the collective and be much more capable of meaningfully contributing to it. And, by the same logic, it is much easier to love others properly when you know how to love yourself. 

I found deep comfort in these things which is why I wanted to write them down, and I hope you will find some comfort in them too.
